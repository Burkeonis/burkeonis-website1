FIELD NOTES / RECOVERY

REGULATION BEFORE RESOLUTION

SEPTEMBER 2026JEFFERY BURKE~5 MIN READ

There is a point in a trigger where more thinking does not create more clarity. Your attention narrows. Your body prepares to fight, escape, freeze, or shut down. Every new detail gets recruited into the threat. Trying to force a breakthrough at that point usually creates damage you will have to repair later.

Regulation is not pretending everything is fine. It is getting enough capacity back to tell the difference between what is happening now and what your nervous system predicts will happen next.

Calm is not the prize. Choice is.

MAP THE FIRST NINETY SECONDS

Start earlier than the explosion. What was the first physical signal—heat, pressure, nausea, a clenched jaw, a need to move, a sudden blankness? What thought arrived with it? What did you do next? This sequence matters because the earlier you recognize it, the more options you still have.

Use interventions that reduce demand instead of adding another performance. Change rooms. Lower sound and light. Put both feet on the floor. Exhale longer than you inhale. Drink water. Say, “I need twenty minutes before I answer this.” The right move is the one you can actually use when capacity is low.

MEASURE THE RECOVERY WINDOW

People often return to the conversation when they are tired of waiting, not when they are ready. Track how long it takes before you can accurately repeat the other person's position, tolerate disagreement, and consider information that does not support your first interpretation. That is a better readiness test than simply feeling quieter.

RESOLVE WHAT REMAINS

Once the threat response drops, the problem may look smaller—or more serious. Both are useful information. Write down the facts, the cost, what you need, what you control, and the next observable action. If the same trigger keeps returning, stop treating each episode as a separate emergency. Map the pattern.

If a reaction feels dangerous, unmanageable, or connected to self-harm, violence, withdrawal, or a medical emergency, a worksheet is not enough. Contact emergency services or a qualified professional who can support you directly.
